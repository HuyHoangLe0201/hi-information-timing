tau_lin sits a median factor 1.00 above its leverage bound,
so on real records the bound is not merely valid but attained.

Two exact bounds bracket the exchangeable case.  The largest of K
non-negative eigenvalues is at least their mean and at most their sum, so
H(c)/K <= lambda_max <= H(c), and with E[H(c)] = cK under exchangeability
the null must satisfy q/K <= tau_lin <= q, here 0.0113 to 0.35.
The shuffled record gives 0.1106, inside the bracket and well above
its lower end; the real record gives 0.0066, sitting ON the lower end.
That is the whole difference, and it is a statement about concentration
rather than about amount, which the next table measures directly.

Both rows are read at the SAME early sample count, the one at which the
real record reaches the bound.  There the real record puts 97% of the
leverage it has spent into a single direction against 47% for the shuffled
one.  The shuffled figure is not small because the window holds fewer
samples than there are channels, so its Gram matrix is rank-deficient and
its mass cannot spread over K directions however unstructured it is; the
comparison that matters is the factor of two between the two rows.

The early advantage of the linear span is therefore a statement about
where the leverage points, not about how much of it there is; the next
table gives the amount for comparison.

Exchangeability spends the budget uniformly, at rate c; the shuffled

the large one.

Leverage is large where the sensitivity points somewhere the rest of the
record does not, so what the span bound finds early is the part of a
bearing's early life least like its own later life.  A linear combination
can be aimed at that; the direction is specific to the record, which the
transfer test below measures.
